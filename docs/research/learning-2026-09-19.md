Follows `learning-2026-09-14.md`, which ended with two findings: a trained
readout on the frozen connectome learns to survive but not to kill, on one

## Route 3: reward-modulated Hebbian learning on the descending neurons' inputs

The selectivity screen of 2026-09-18 showed where the game does reach:
58 descending types receive vision through the measured wiring, and the
reflex pilot reads five of them. So the same three-factor rule
(`plasticity.py`, the 2026-09-14 machinery, unchanged for the mushroom
body) is applied to the synapses onto the descending neurons:

| edge set | edges | what |
|---|---|---|
| `vis_dn` | 24,110 | presynaptic cell a visual projection or visual centrifugal neuron, postsynaptic a descending neuron |
| `dn_in` | 332,213 | every measured input to a descending neuron |
| `<set>_shuffled` | same count | random edges touching neither the set's sources nor its targets: the rule with the same statistics on synapses that cannot matter to steering |

The rule on these sets differs from the mushroom-body rule in two stated
ways. Eligibility is Hebbian (presynaptic trace times postsynaptic trace,
both 0.5 s), so an input is credited only when its descending neuron
fired; the mushroom-body rule was presynaptic-only, as in the fly, where
dopamine acts on the Kenyon-cell terminals. The change is relative to the
measured weight, `dw = eta * eligibility * dopamine * |w0|`, bounded to
0.1-2.0 times the measured value and never changing sign. The eligibility
is heavy-tailed (measured on `vis_dn` over 600 ticks: mean 6, 90th
percentile 18, maximum above 10,000) and is capped at 50; with eta 2e-5 a
kill or a hit (six 100 Hz ticks of PAM or PPL1) moves a typically
co-active synapse by about 10%, a maximally co-active one by up to 60%.
Reward-modulated Hebbian learning of this form is the spiking version of
a policy gradient (Fremaux and Gerstner 2016), which is what "RL inside
the brain" can honestly mean here.

The dopamine term is unchanged: the fly's own PAM population rate minus
its PPL1 rate, driven by the game's teaching pulses (a kill pulses PAM, a
hit pulses PPL1), centred on a 20 s running mean. Those neurons do not
contact the descending neurons' inputs in the wiring; the signal is
applied to them as a global modulator and everything that shows it is
labelled ENGINEERED. Aminergic synapses inside the sets carry zero fast
weight under the corrected brain and cannot move (0.1 x 0 to 2 x 0).

Protocol, pre-registered before the runs: the reflex pilot (`pool4`,
saccade hold 3, startle refractory 15, LI steering) plays 24 episodes of
900 ticks on each of four flies sharing one weight matrix; conditions are
rule on, rule off (`lr0`, everything computed, nothing applied) and rule
on the shuffled set, at two seeds for `vis_dn` and one for `dn_in`. The
learning measure is kills and lives lost per episode, first half against
second half. The measure that matters more is the frozen test: the
learned weights installed in a fresh brain with the rule off, on four
game seeds none of the runs saw (700-703), 12 episodes each, against the
measured weights on the same seeds. A brain that learned should score
differently from the measured one there; a brain whose changes were noise
should not.

## Route 1 again: three seeds and a kill-shaped reward

`train_readout.py --life-penalty 0.1` makes a kill worth ten lives
instead of two, the setting the 2026-09-14 note asked for; three training
seeds (1, 2, 3) per condition (connectome features, rewired features,
retina features), 400 updates of 128 ticks on eight flies each, 410k ticks
per run as before, then greedy evaluation on the four held-out seeds
against random play and the reflex pilot. The question is unchanged: is
the measured wiring a better feature map than a random graph with the
same degrees, once the seed spread is known?

## In the pilot

`run_m3.py --learned-weights out/plasticity_<tag>_weights.npz` installs a
learned set into the pilot's brain (checked against the saved measured
weights, so a file from another wiring or correction is refused) and
prints LEARNED WEIGHTS with the count of changed synapses on the footer.
`run_m3.py --learn vis_dn` runs the rule live while the fly plays: the
footer shows the fraction of synapses moved and the mean drift, and the
weights are saved at the end. Both are labelled on every frame.

The rule learns, and what it learns is harmful. Both brains trained on the
visual inputs to the descending neurons kill less and die more than the
measured brain on games none of them saw, by about three standard errors
on deaths; the brain trained on every descending input is the worst, at
under half the measured kill rate. The size-matched shuffled controls,
which move as many synapses by as much on edges that do not end on a
descending neuron, are not worse on kills (if anything slightly better,
within the error) and mixed on deaths. So the harm is specific to the
synapses the reflex pilot reads through: the rule is acting on the right
substrate, and acting wrongly.

### What it did

`scripts/audit_learned_weights.py` (full tables in
`out/audit_learned_weights_2026-09-19.txt`). In both seeds the rule
depresses the loom pathway: the synapses from the loom detectors LC4 and
LPLC4 onto the giant fibre DNp01 and the rest of the loom pool (DNp02,
DNp04, DNp12) fall to 0.3-0.7 of their measured weight, with 106 of the
118 visual inputs to DNp02 down in both seeds (one up in seed 0, none in seed 1). The seeds
differ elsewhere (seed 0 also strengthens the DNa02 and DNg100 inputs by
a third; seed 1, with a hit-heavier history, depresses almost everything,
total weight 0.79 of measured), but the loom-pool depression is shared,
and the frozen test says it costs lives. On `dn_in` the same happens to
every readout pool at once (inputs to DNa02, DNg100, DNp01, DNp04, DNb05,
DNp07 all at 0.6-0.8 of measured) and the fly barely plays.

The reason is credit assignment, and it is the interesting part. A hit in
Battlezone comes at the end of a loom. In the half-second before it the
synapses that are co-active are exactly the ones carrying the threat to
the escape neurons, and the rule's behavioural sign convention,
"punishment weakens what was active", weakens them. But their activity
was the correct response, not the cause of the hit; the fly was hit
because the escape was late or the tank was faster, and a three-factor
rule with a half-second eligibility cannot tell the difference. Reward
does the mirror: a kill strengthens whatever fired before it, which in
seed 0 is the steer and drive inputs, but kills are three times rarer
than hits here, so on balance the rule dismantles the reflex it was
given. This is the same shape as the 2026-09-14 learning result (readouts
learn to die less, never to kill more), seen from the other side: the
game's reinforcers are informative about survival, and survival is what
the measured wiring already does through the loom route.

A follow-up separates the two signals: the same rule with only kills
teaching (PAM only) and with only hits teaching (PPL1 only), two seeds
each, frozen-tested the same way. If the account above were the whole
story, the hits-only brains should reproduce the harm and the kills-only
brains should not.

### Kills only, hits only

| brain (vis_dn, frozen test on games 700-703) | kills per episode | lives lost per episode | total weight vs measured | loom pool (DNp02 inputs) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| measured weights | 0.85 ± 0.15 | 1.90 ± 0.18 | 1.000 | 1.00 |
| hits only, seed 0 | 0.65 ± 0.12 | 2.52 ± 0.16 | 0.811 | 0.27 |
| hits only, seed 1 | 0.71 ± 0.12 | 2.60 ± 0.16 | 0.773 | 0.25 |
| kills only, seed 0 | 0.71 ± 0.12 | 2.62 ± 0.17 | 1.041 | 0.95 |
| kills only, seed 1 | 0.81 ± 0.12 | 2.77 ± 0.18 | 1.089 | 1.14 |

Half right. Hits alone do what the mixed rule did: nine in ten moved
synapses go down, the loom pool's inputs fall to a quarter, the fly dies
more. But kills alone, which strengthen 60-80% of what they touch (DNa02
inputs up 15-22%, DNp01 up 10%, DNp02 up 14% in seed 1) and leave the
loom pool intact, make the fly die just as much, at 2.6-2.8 lives per
episode against 1.9, with kills unchanged. Potentiating the steer, drive
and escape inputs together is as bad for survival as depressing the
escape inputs alone.

So the credit-assignment account explains the hits-only brains, and a
plainer one covers all of them: the reflex controller is calibrated to
the measured descending rates (the `pool4` spans, noted as provisional
since 2026-09-14), and the rule, whichever way it pushes, rescales those
rates. Every rule-on brain on the real sets, six of six across mixed,
hits-only and kills-only teaching, dies more on the frozen test; the
shuffled controls, which leave the descending inputs alone, do not. With
the downstream readout fixed, a three-factor rule on the descending
neurons' inputs has no direction that helps: it is a gain perturbation
of a tuned reflex, and the game's reinforcers do not tell it which gains
to keep. Learning that could help would have to move the readout with
the synapses (which is what Route 1 does, with the synapses fixed) or
re-calibrate the controller as the rates drift, and either is a
different experiment.

What this does establish, for the first time in this project: a
plasticity rule that reaches the game changes behaviour reproducibly and
in a way the wiring audit predicts (loom inputs down, escape worse),
where the mushroom-body rule could not reach it at all. The substrate
learns; what it is taught is the problem.

## 2026-09-20: is it the calibration? The learned brains under an adaptive readout

The reading above blames the fixed readout: the `pool4` spans map each
descending pool's rate to a drive on 0-1 with numbers taken from the
measured brain, so any brain whose descending rates are rescaled crosses
the startle, fire and drive thresholds at the wrong moments. That is a
testable claim. `CockpitControls(calibration="adaptive")` replaces the
fixed spans with each pool's own running peak rate (30 s decay, 4 Hz
floor, starting at the floor so the brain's first events set the scale),
and scales the lateralisation-index gate with the steer pools' range;
nothing else in the controller changes. A unit test shows the fixed
calibration losing the startle when every rate is halved and the adaptive
one keeping it.

The frozen test is repeated under it: the measured weights and the six
`vis_dn` brains (mixed, kills-only, hits-only teaching, two seeds each),
rule off, games 700-703, 12 episodes per fly, four flies. If the harm was
the calibration, the learned brains should now match the measured brain;
if the rule damaged the computation itself (the loom pathway at a quarter
of its weight is still a quarter, whatever the span), the hits-trained
brains at least should still die more. The measured spans each brain
settles on are written to `out/plasticity_<tag>_peaks.json`.

|---|---|---|---|
| measured | 54 / 53 | 64 | 32 |

Two findings. First, the adaptive readout does not rescue any learned
brain: all six stay at or below the measured brain on kills and above it
on deaths under the same readout. The gaps narrow only because the
measured brain itself falls, from 0.85 to 0.52 kills, under a readout
that starts every span at the floor and lets early noise set it: the
provisional `pool4` spans carry a prior that matters, and the adaptive
mode is the worse controller. Second, and settling the question, the
giant fibre's own running peak is 32 Hz in the measured brain and sits at
the floor in every brain trained with hits: DNp01 no longer fires above
4 Hz in a hundred seconds of play. The loom pathway is not rescaled, it is
gone, and no readout calibration can read a signal that is not there. The
kills-only brains keep a little of it (8 Hz) and lose most of the steer
pools' range instead.

So the 2026-09-19 reading is corrected: the harm is not the readout's
calibration but the rule's credit assignment, on both sides. Punishment
after a loom removes the loom response; reward after a kill rescales the
steer and drive inputs away from the regime the reflex works in. A
three-factor rule with a sensory eligibility trace cannot learn this
reflex better than the wiring already has it, because the reinforcers
arrive after the correct response and are attributed to it. What could
learn here is a rule whose eligibility is tied to the fly's own actions
rather than its inputs (credit the turn, not the sight of the tank), or
a readout learned jointly with the synapses. Both are open; neither is a
tweak.

## 2026-09-20, later: the dopamine as a prediction error

The 2026-09-19 conclusion was credit assignment: a hit arrives at the end
of a loom and the rule punishes the escape that was already under way; a
kill rewards whatever fired before it. Both are what a raw reinforcer does
to a synapse with a half-second memory. The fly's dopamine neurons do not
carry the raw reinforcer; they carry something closer to a prediction
error, and that is the standard cure for exactly this failure: a hit that
the sensory state already predicted teaches nothing, and an escape that
averts a predicted hit is rewarded, because the outcome was better than
predicted.

`train_plasticity.py --dopamine rpe`: a linear critic V(x) = w.x over the
squad's pooled features (the same 907 log-rates of the descending,
visual-projection and visual-centrifugal types the PPO readout uses),
trained online by TD(0) with gamma 0.97 and learning rate 1e-3 on the
squad's mean, shared by the four flies; each tick the prediction error
delta = r + gamma V(x') - V(x) with r a kill minus half a hit, divided by
its running RMS, drives the teaching Poisson rates: PAM at 100 Hz per
unit of positive error, PPL1 per unit of negative, no event pulses. The
three-factor rule, the edge set (`vis_dn`), the eligibility, the bounds
and eta are unchanged from the 19th, so the only difference between this
run and the mixed-teaching run is what the dopamine neurons are told.
The critic is engineered and labelled so; the fly's own critic would be
the mushroom body, which cannot see this game.

Protocol as before: two seeds, 24 episodes of 900 ticks on four flies,
the frozen test on games 700-703 against the measured weights. If credit
assignment was the whole problem, the learned brains should now not die
more than the measured brain, and might do better; if the readout's
fixed calibration limits any weight change, they should still be worse.

### Result: the critic did not learn, and the brains died more again

| brain (vis_dn, frozen test on games 700-703, 48 episodes) | kills per episode | lives lost per episode |
|---|---|---|
| measured weights | 0.85 ± 0.15 | 1.90 ± 0.18 |
| events dopamine, seeds 0 / 1 (2026-09-19) | 0.69 ± 0.11 / 0.69 ± 0.13 | 2.56 ± 0.16 / 2.67 ± 0.19 |
| RPE dopamine, seeds 0 / 1 | 0.85 ± 0.13 / 0.60 ± 0.11 | 2.60 ± 0.18 / 2.62 ± 0.18 |

The RPE brains die as much as the events brains, and the weight audit
says why: the loom pathway is depressed again (LC4 inputs to the loom
pool at 0.85 of measured in seed 0 and 0.45 in seed 1, DNp02's inputs at
0.91 and 0.33), which is the events result. The critic never predicted
anything: its weight norm ended at 0.011 and 0.016, so V was zero and the
prediction error was the raw reinforcer minus a running mean, which is
the events rule. The unnormalised TD(0) step of 1e-3 over 907 features
was far too small.

Two more critics were tried on the side, in three-episode probes with
the rule off, watching the error at the moment of a hit (a critic that
has learned to expect a hit from the loom that precedes it shows a small
error there; an ignorant one shows the full -0.5): normalised LMS with a
step of 0.5 on standardised features learns something (norm 0.5 to 3)
but noisily, with the error at hits only falling from -0.79 to about
-0.5 in twelve episodes and the teaching rates turned into a continuous
25-30 Hz noise on both dopamine populations; a step of 0.1 does not move
(norm 0.02, error at hits -0.33 to -0.45). A linear map over the pooled
population does not predict this game's hits one to two seconds ahead
within the episodes we can afford, even though the loom pool's own rates
are among its inputs. The critic implementation is kept with the
normalised step as its default and the error-at-hits logged, for whoever
tries a structured predictor (the loom pool alone, or a longer horizon
and many more episodes).

So the in-brain learning question closes today where it was: the rule
reaches the substrate and moves it reproducibly; every teaching signal
tried, raw events, kills only, hits only, and a prediction error from a
critic that could not learn, leaves the fly dying more than the measured
wiring does. Credit assignment is still the diagnosis; a signal that
actually carries it has not been built.

### The structured critic, and what the traces say about hits

A critic over the readout pools' own rates and their one-second
low-passes (`--critic structured`, ten features) does no better: in a
twelve-episode probe its error at the moment of a hit stays at -0.42 to
-0.44 of a full -0.5. The traces of the recorded fights say why no
critic can do better. In the fifteen ticks before a hit the descending
pools are silent: DNp01 at 0.3 Hz against a baseline of 6, DNa02 at 0-2
against 15, pIP10 at 0.2 against 14 (seed 0, female, three recordings).
A hit in Battlezone is an enemy shell, small and fast, and this retina
and brain do not see it coming; the fly is hit while looking at nothing.
Before a kill it is the reverse: DNa02 at 126-138 Hz, DNp01 at 45, the
tank close and looming, the whole readout ablaze. So a kill is
predictable and a hit is not, and a prediction error cannot rescue the
punishment side.

That also corrects the mechanism given on the 19th. The loom pathway was
not depressed because a hit follows a loom; before a hit there is no
loom in the pools. It was depressed because the punishment pulse lasts
six ticks after the hit, and in those ticks the brain is responding to
the hit's own consequence, the screen's damage flash, which drives the
loom detectors; the rule punished the response to the flash. The
eligibility of the rule looks half a second back, and on the punishment
side that half second is filled by the event itself.

The fix that follows is in the rule, not the signal: latch the
eligibility as it stood the tick before the reinforcer and apply the
pulse to that (`MBPlasticity(latch=True)`, `--latch`), so what is
credited or blamed is what preceded the outcome. On the punishment side
that is, by the traces, nothing; on the reward side it is the approach
and the shot. Two seeds on `vis_dn`, events dopamine, frozen test as
before. Results below.

### Result: with the eligibility latched, the learned brain is better than the measured one

| brain (vis_dn, frozen test on games 700-703, 48 episodes) | kills per episode | lives lost per episode |
|---|---|---|
| measured weights | 0.85 ± 0.15 | 1.90 ± 0.18 |
| events dopamine, unlatched, seed 0 (2026-09-19) | 0.69 ± 0.11 | 2.56 ± 0.16 |
| events dopamine, latched, seed 0 | 0.85 ± 0.14 | 1.38 ± 0.14 |
| events dopamine, latched, seed 1 | 1.08 ± 0.15 | 1.60 ± 0.19 |

For the first time in this project a brain that learned plays better on
games it never saw than the measured wiring does: both latched seeds die
less (1.38 and 1.60 lives per episode against 1.90, about two standard
errors each) and kill as often or more (0.85 and 1.08 against 0.85). The
only change from the 19th's harmful rule is when the dopamine looks: at
the eligibility that preceded the reinforcer, not at the brain's response
to the reinforcer's own flash.

The audit says what it learned. Nearly everything the rule touched went
up (8,299 of 9,696 moved synapses in seed 0, 8,721 of 9,711 in seed 1):
the steer inputs (DNa02 at 1.49 and 1.62 of measured), the drive
(DNg100 at 1.56 and 1.69), the loom pool (DNp02 at 1.46 and 1.89, DNp04
at 1.29 and 1.54, LC4 onto the pool at 1.13 and 1.67). Kills credit the
approach and the shot; hits, arriving from a shell the retina never
carries, have no eligible synapse to blame, and the latch keeps them from
blaming the flash. The result is a brain with a stronger steer, a
stronger drive and a stronger escape, and it survives better because of
the last.

The kills-only latched control, which by the account above should match
(punishment has nothing to act on), does not match cleanly:

| brain (latched, frozen test) | kills per episode | lives lost per episode |
|---|---|---|
| mixed teaching, seeds 0 / 1 | 0.85 / 1.08 | 1.38 / 1.60 |
| kills only, seeds 0 / 1 | 1.17 / 0.77 | 1.92 / 2.33 |

Its weights look the same (potentiation nearly everywhere, DNa02 inputs
at 1.27-1.45, DNp02 at 1.32-1.67, LC4 onto the pool at 1.24-1.54), one
seed kills more than any brain here (1.17) and neither dies less than
the measured 1.90. Either the hit pulse, latched, still does something
useful with the little that precedes a hit (a mild check on the loom
pool's potentiation), or two seeds per arm cannot separate 1.4-1.6 from
1.9-2.3 lives. Two more mixed seeds were queued to tell.

### Four seeds: the latch makes the rule harmless, not helpful

| brain (latched, mixed teaching, frozen test on games 700-703) | kills per episode | lives lost per episode |
|---|---|---|
| measured weights | 0.85 ± 0.15 | 1.90 ± 0.18 |
| seed 0 | 0.85 ± 0.14 | 1.38 ± 0.14 |
| seed 1 | 1.08 ± 0.15 | 1.60 ± 0.19 |
| seed 2 | 0.65 ± 0.12 | 2.48 ± 0.15 |
| seed 3 | 0.79 ± 0.13 | 2.50 ± 0.17 |
| all four pooled (192 episodes) | 0.84 ± 0.07 | 1.99 ± 0.09 |

Seeds 2 and 3 die more than the measured brain, by as much as seeds 0
and 1 died less, and pooled over four seeds the latched brains are the
measured brain: 0.84 kills and 1.99 lives against 0.85 and 1.90. The
"first learned brain that beats the wiring" of two seeds ago was the
upper half of a spread. What survives is smaller and still real: on the
19th every rule that reached the substrate made the fly worse, six
brains of six; with the latch, four brains of four are on average no
different from the measured wiring, two better and two worse. The rule
no longer damages what it touches. It does not yet improve it, and with
seed spreads of 1.4 to 2.5 lives per episode on the same games, showing
an improvement would take many more seeds than a rule that only
potentiates the approach is likely to earn.

### The second held-out set

| brain (latched, frozen test on games 800-803) | kills per episode | lives lost per episode |
|---|---|---|
| measured weights | 1.04 ± 0.14 | 2.25 ± 0.18 |
| seed 0 | 0.94 ± 0.14 | 1.52 ± 0.16 |
| seed 1 | 1.15 ± 0.18 | 1.50 ± 0.14 |
| seed 2 | 0.71 ± 0.12 | 2.56 ± 0.16 |
| seed 3 | 0.75 ± 0.12 | 2.40 ± 0.13 |
| all four pooled (192 episodes) | 0.89 ± 0.07 | 1.99 ± 0.08 |

The per-seed pattern repeats on games none of the brains or the first
test saw: seeds 0 and 1 die less than the measured brain on both sets
(1.38 and 1.60, then 1.52 and 1.50, against 1.90 and 2.25), seeds 2 and
3 die more on both (2.48 and 2.50, then 2.56 and 2.40). That is not
evaluation noise; it is four different brains, two of which learned
something that survives a change of games and two of which learned
something that does not. The pooled number is again the measured brain,
0.89 kills and 1.99 lives against 1.04 and 2.25. What the learning
history did differently in seeds 0 and 1 is the next question, and the
weight audits, which look alike at the level of pools, do not answer it;
it would take a per-synapse comparison of the two pairs.

### The shell, at the fly's resolution

`scripts/probe_shell.py` and a window sweep on seed 0 answer whether the
shell could be seen at all. On the game frame it is a white square of a
few pixels that leaves the tank and grows down the middle of the screen
for about a dozen ticks; in the eye render it is plainly there. On the
retina it is not: with the pilot's window (zoom 2, centred) no ommatidium
changes by more than 0.15 in the twelve ticks before any of five hits,
and with the whole screen in view (zoom 1, one ommatidium per hundred
pixels of the render) none either, in three hits. Only a window aimed
below centre (zoom 2, centre at 0.62 of the height) catches it, in one
hit of four, over the last eight ticks, when it is close and large. The
retina has 721 ommatidia per eye, about what a fly has, and the shell is
smaller than one of them until it is nearly home. The readout pools,
means over hundreds of cells, could not have seen a one-ommatidium event
anyway.

So the hits in this game are unpredictable to this fly for the same
reason a real fly would not see a pellet: resolution. That is why the
punishment side of learning could never carry credit, why the critics
failed, and why the latch, which gives punishment nothing to act on,
was the fix. A higher-resolution retina would be a different animal.

### What separates the two pairs

A synapse-by-synapse comparison of the four latched brains (same edge
set, log ratio of learned to measured weight; `out/latch_pair_comparison.npz`):

- The two better brains resemble each other more (correlation of their
  log ratios 0.71) than the two worse ones do each other (0.53) or than
  either pair resembles the other (0.28-0.49).
- The steer and drive inputs went up alike in both pairs: DNa02's visual
  inputs at 1.36 of measured in the better pair, 1.32 in the worse.
- The loom-to-escape pathway went opposite ways. Inputs to the loom
  pool from LC4 and LPLC4 are potentiated in the better pair and
  depressed in the worse: DNp02 at 1.61 against 0.32, DNp05 at 1.22
  against 0.47, DNp12 at 1.41 against 0.64, DNp04 at 1.25 against 0.68,
  DNp01 at 1.09 against 0.68; by presynaptic type, LC4's outputs at 1.21
  against 0.56. Nothing else in the top of the list comes close.
- The histories differ the way that predicts: seed 2 took the most hits
  in training (2.61 per episode against 1.84-2.21) and had the highest
  PPL1 rate (9.3 Hz against 5.9-7.7).

So the latch does not remove the punishment of the escape route; it
weakens it, and whether the escape ends up stronger or weaker than it
started depends on how many hits the history dealt. Two seeds came out
with a stronger escape and survive better on games they never saw; two
came out with a weaker one and survive worse. The prediction is causal
and cheap to test: take the measured brain, scale only the LC4 and LPLC4
synapses onto the loom pool by 1.5 and by 0.5, and frozen-test both on
both held-out sets. If survival follows the escape gain, the whole
learning result reduces to one number the rule happens to move.

### The scaling test: not that one number

| brain (frozen test) | games 700-703, kills / lives | games 800-803, kills / lives |
|---|---|---|
| measured weights | 0.85 / 1.90 | 1.04 / 2.25 |
| measured, LC4 and LPLC4 onto the loom pool x1.5 (483 synapses) | 0.88 / 2.08 | 1.06 / 1.77 |
| measured, the same x0.5 | 1.04 / 2.04 | 1.02 / 1.98 |
| latched seed 0 | 0.85 / 1.38 | 0.94 / 1.52 |
| latched seed 2 | 0.65 / 2.48 | 0.71 / 2.56 |

Scaling only the loom pathway in the measured brain reproduces neither
outcome: up by half is no better on one set and better on the other,
down by half is not worse on either. The 483 synapses that separate the
pairs most in the audit are not, on their own, what makes seed 0 survive
and seed 2 die. Whatever the two pairs learned is distributed over the
rest of the 9,700 synapses each moved, and the loom pathway's sign is a
marker of the history, not the cause of the outcome. The next test swaps
just that pathway between seed 0 and seed 2 (chimeras), which tells
whether the pathway matters in the context of the rest of the learned
pattern; results below.

### The pathway swap: the survival pattern stays with the recipient brain

Recovered during the 2026-09-22 handoff from the existing experiment logs.
All four runs in `out/run_stage12.sh` finished with exit code 0 on

| latched seed 0 | 0.85 / 1.38 | 0.94 / 1.52 |

### Evaluation protocol audit, 2026-09-22

Reviewing the implementation for the repository write-up found that the
September 18 random-start fix applies to `scripts/bench_pilots.py`, but not
to `src/noflyzone/rl_env.py`. This affects the learning comparisons above
and the initial PPO note; it does not remove the explicit warm-up from the
later pilot benchmarks.

`FlySquad` passes its seed list to `PilotLoop`, which passes each seed to
the Gym reset in `BattleZone`. It performs no seeded action warm-up. Its
`make_brain("cuda", batch=...)` call does not forward the training seed,
so the neural RNG uses the backend's default seed. Per-fly random offsets
still exist, and GPU accumulation can cause trajectory divergence; neither
establishes the claimed independent training seeds.

A direct check used the current `BattleZone` wrapper and an identical
450-action sequence from `numpy.random.default_rng(123)` for each of
700, 701, 702, 703, 800, 801, 802 and 803. The initial frame and every
subsequent frame were hashed in sequence. **All eight SHA-256 hashes are
identical**, and all runs scored 1,000 with three life losses. The action
sequence, package versions and resulting hashes are saved in
[the seed audit](evidence/seed-audit-2026-09-22.json). This checks emulator
start diversity, not equivalence of full closed-loop neural trajectories.

There is a second dependence: at the 900-tick episode boundary, `FlySquad`
resets the episode counters but keeps the world running. Neural rows reset
only at game over. A table containing 48 logged episodes therefore need not
contain 48 independent game restarts, and the historical standard errors
do not account for that dependence.

The observed weights, behaviour and pathway interventions remain recorded
measurements. The repeated better/worse pattern is descriptive evidence;
these runs do not establish generalisation to independently held-out games.
The 483-connection swap did not transfer the observed survival pattern
under the recorded protocol. It should be rerun after correcting the
evaluation, rather than treated as a definitive causal null.

The [final-learning evidence bundle](evidence/learning-final-2026-09-22.json)
contains the original per-episode rows for both evaluation labels of the
measured baseline, four latched brains and two chimeras, plus source-file
hashes and recomputed means. The full raw archive remains local. No neural
training or frozen evaluation was rerun during this write-up, and no code
was changed. The new execution was the small emulator seed check above.

Before another learning claim: propagate and record independent neural
and game randomisation, define true reset versus continuous-window episodes,
and compare learned weights to the measured baseline under matched, verified
new starts. Use independent training histories as the unit for assessing
variation between learned brains.
